Limited Scrutiny vs Complete Scrutiny: Can an AO Make Additions Beyond CASS Reasons?

T Tanooj
Tax Practitioner / GST Practitioner 12 Oct 20268 min read

Recently, there has been a noticeable increase in taxpayers relying on the Limited Scrutiny vs Complete Scrutiny argument in their submissions before Assessing Officers (AOs) during assessment proceedings under Section 143(3) of the Income-tax Act, 1961.

The argument is often straightforward: “My income tax return was selected for scrutiny for a specific reason. Therefore, the Assessing Officer cannot make additions on any other issue unless the case is converted from Limited Scrutiny to Complete Scrutiny.”

An assessing officer responding to the argument of the Assesee provides an important clarification on this issue. The Department rejected the assessee's objection on the ground that the case was not selected under the Limited Scrutiny category and that the scope of assessment was not restricted to the original CASS selection reason.

For taxpayers currently facing scrutiny assessments, the distinction between Limited Scrutiny and Complete Scrutiny can be crucial. Before raising an objection that an addition is beyond the scope of scrutiny, it is important to establish whether the assessment is actually classified as Limited Scrutiny.

1. What is the Limited Scrutiny vs Complete Scrutiny argument?

When a return of income is selected for scrutiny under the Computer Assisted Scrutiny Selection (CASS) mechanism, the selection may be associated with a particular issue or parameter, such as a substantial deduction claimed by the taxpayer, a transaction requiring verification, or a discrepancy identified by the Department.

This often leads taxpayers to question whether the Assessing Officer can examine issues unrelated to the original selection reason.

The objection generally takes the following form:

  • The return was selected for scrutiny for a specific CASS parameter.

  • The proposed addition relates to an issue outside that parameter.

  • The assessment was not formally converted from Limited Scrutiny to Complete Scrutiny.

  • Therefore, the proposed addition is alleged to be beyond the permissible scope of assessment.

Although this argument may be relevant in an appropriate Limited Scrutiny case, the mere existence of a specific CASS selection reason does not, by itself, establish that the assessment is Limited Scrutiny.

The actual scrutiny classification and the applicable instructions governing the assessment must be examined before drawing that conclusion.

2. Income Tax Department rejects the objection

In a communication, the Assessment Unit of the Income Tax Department rejected an assessee's contention that proposed additions beyond the original scrutiny parameter were impermissible.

The case concerned Assessment Year 2025–26. The stated reason for selection was a large deduction claimed under Section 80E of the Income-tax Act under the old tax regime.

The assessee argued that the assessment should be confined to verifying the deduction under Section 80E and that additions concerning unrelated issues could not be made without conversion from Limited Scrutiny to Complete Scrutiny.

The Department rejected this contention for a decisive reason: the case had not been selected under the Limited Scrutiny category.

The communication recorded that the Limited Scrutiny flag on the ITBA portal was marked “No”. It further stated that, for the relevant assessment year, cases selected under CASS were subject to Complete Scrutiny and that the assessment was consequently not confined to the issue relating to Section 80E.

The Department also rejected the contention that the applicable guidelines for compulsory selection of returns for Complete Scrutiny during FY 2026–27 established a restriction on the scope of CASS-selected cases. It clarified that those guidelines dealt with compulsory selection criteria and were distinct from the CASS selection process.

Further, the Assessee has also obtained confirmations from various JAOs through RTI whether the concept of limited scrutiny is relevant and valid. However, the Department observed that the RTI response relied upon by the assessee did not establish a prohibition on conducting Complete Scrutiny in cases selected through CASS. The assessee's objection was accordingly rejected.

3. Can an Assessing Officer make additions beyond the CASS selection reason?

This is the central question for taxpayers searching for “addition beyond CASS selection reason” or “Can AO make additions beyond limited scrutiny?”

The answer depends on the scrutiny category and the legal framework applicable to the particular assessment.

Case 1: The assessment is classified as Complete Scrutiny

Where the case is selected for Complete Scrutiny, the taxpayer cannot automatically restrict the Assessing Officer's examination to the specific issue mentioned in the CASS selection reason.

In the communication discussed above, the Department specifically rejected the argument that the original selection reason under Section 80E restricted the scope of assessment, because the ITBA portal did not identify the case as Limited Scrutiny.

Accordingly, an objection based solely on the fact that a proposed addition falls outside the original CASS selection reason may not succeed in such circumstances.

Case 2: The assessment is genuinely classified as Limited Scrutiny

A different analysis applies where the case is actually selected under Limited Scrutiny.

In such cases, the prescribed restrictions on the scope of examination and the applicable procedure for expanding the scope of scrutiny become relevant. The taxpayer should examine the applicable CBDT instructions and whether the prescribed conditions and approvals for expansion of scope, where required, have been complied with.

The Department's communication does not establish that the restrictions applicable to genuine Limited Scrutiny cases have been abolished.

Therefore, the distinction is not simply whether the proposed addition relates to the original CASS reason. The first question is whether the case is Limited Scrutiny or Complete Scrutiny.

4. How to verify whether your income tax scrutiny is Limited Scrutiny or Complete Scrutiny

Before making a submission challenging the jurisdiction of the Assessing Officer, taxpayers and their authorised representatives should verify the actual classification of the assessment.

The following checks can help:

Step 1: Examine the scrutiny notice and related communications.

Review the notices issued in the assessment proceedings and identify the stated scrutiny category and selection reason, where disclosed.

Step 2: Distinguish the selection reason from the scrutiny category.

A CASS selection reason identifies the issue that triggered scrutiny. It should not be treated as conclusive evidence that the case falls under Limited Scrutiny.

Step 3: Review the applicable CBDT instructions.

Where the case is classified as Limited Scrutiny, examine the instructions applicable to the relevant assessment year, including the procedure governing examination of issues beyond the original scope.

Step 4: Frame the objection on the facts and applicable law.

If the proposed addition is alleged to be outside the permissible scope of assessment, the objection should be supported by the actual scrutiny classification, the relevant departmental communications and the applicable legal provisions or instructions.

5. Does the absence of conversion from Limited Scrutiny to Complete Scrutiny invalidate an addition?

The absence of a formal conversion from Limited Scrutiny to Complete Scrutiny is not, by itself, sufficient to establish that an addition is invalid.

The question arises only after determining whether the case was actually selected under Limited Scrutiny and whether the issue examined falls outside its permissible scope.

In the case discussed above, the Department rejected the conversion argument because it considered the case to be one of Complete Scrutiny from the outset. The issue, therefore, was not whether a Limited Scrutiny case had been validly converted, but whether the original classification permitted examination beyond the selection reason.

Conversely, where a case is genuinely classified as Limited Scrutiny, the applicable restrictions and prescribed procedures cannot simply be disregarded on the basis of this individual communication.

It is therefore important not to interpret the Department's clarification as a blanket rule validating every addition made during scrutiny proceedings.

6. What should tax consultants do when drafting a reply to a Section 143(3) notice?

For tax consultants handling scrutiny assessments, the Limited Scrutiny vs Complete Scrutiny objection should be evaluated before it is included in a reply to a show-cause notice.

A sound assessment response should distinguish between:

  • Jurisdictional objections: Whether the Assessing Officer is acting within the permissible scope of the assessment and has complied with applicable procedural requirements.

  • Merits-based objections: Whether the proposed addition is factually and legally sustainable.

  • Procedural objections: Whether the relevant statutory provisions, notices, opportunities and applicable departmental instructions have been complied with.

Where the scrutiny category does not support a Limited Scrutiny objection, repeating the argument that the addition falls outside the original CASS selection reason may distract from stronger arguments on the merits of the proposed addition.

Equally, where the assessment is genuinely Limited Scrutiny, the taxpayer should not abandon a potentially relevant procedural objection merely because the Department has rejected such an argument in a case with a different scrutiny classification.

The objective should be to build a case-specific response rather than rely on a standard paragraph that is reproduced across multiple assessment submissions.

Conclusion: Verify the scrutiny classification before raising the objection

The Income Tax Department's communication dated 30 September 2026 highlights an important distinction in scrutiny assessment proceedings: the reason for selection under CASS is not necessarily the same as the permissible scope of assessment.

Taxpayers facing additions beyond the original scrutiny reason should first verify whether their cases are classified as Limited Scrutiny or Complete Scrutiny. The answer to that question can materially affect the relevance of an objection concerning conversion from Limited Scrutiny to Complete Scrutiny.

For tax professionals, the takeaway is clear: do not rely on the CASS selection reason alone to establish that an Assessing Officer has exceeded the permissible scope of scrutiny. Examine the actual classification, applicable CBDT instructions and procedural record before advising the taxpayer.

At the same time, this individual communication should not be read as a general abolition of safeguards applicable to genuine Limited Scrutiny cases. The correct position must be determined with reference to the facts and applicable law in each assessment.
